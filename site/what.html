What the gear boxes can do

2026-10-26.

Short answer

A row of boxes is a dataflow program over one continuous variable, shaft angle, and each box is a pure function from its input angle to its output angle. That makes the whole system an analog computer of the same family as Vannevar Bush's differential analyzer: it can scale, add, negate, route, integrate (count), quantize and look up fixed functions, and with a governor or escapement it can run feedback loops and clocks. It cannot branch on a value or multiply two variables without a part that is a project of its own, so the fun is in physical signal processing, not logic.

Three headline answers:

  1. What can it do? Everything linear, plus fixed functions in plastic (cams) and physical dynamics (inertia, springs, friction) that software can only simulate. Constants are drivable: a compile-time constant is a tooth ratio, a run-time constant is a second input held by a latch and fed into an adder. Sections 2 to 4 give the model, a catalog of 14 boxes and five machines.
  2. How far can one drive reach? Three boxes is about right for a hand crank when a reading must be accurate, and too conservative for anything that just has to move. With unlubricated PLA, module 1 and the current 0.2 mm pin play, expect a crank to turn 6 to 8 boxes and a small 9 V gearmotor 4 to 6, with position readings drifting by about a third of a digit per three boxes from backlash. A torque budget rule, a power line on N/S and a drop of dry lube are the three fixes, in that order. Section 5 has the numbers.
  3. The look. Color by role: red where energy comes in, blue where a signal enters a box, orange where it leaves, green where you read it, bone for the shell and white for the gears nobody needs to notice. Section 6 says why those six and how they fit the four-slot AMS.

Rotation as a signal, boxes as functions

The signal on a lane is one real number per instant, the angle θ(t) of the port gear, and its derivative is the speed ω(t). The standard already fixes the units: what crosses an edge is pitch-line travel in millimeters, forward is output counter-clockwise, and a mesh passes the value on unchanged. So every lane is a typed wire carrying one float, E/W and N/S are two incompatible types (different layers), and the three lanes on a side are a three-wire cable. There is no clock and no sampling; the program runs at the speed you turn it.

Most boxes are linear, and the same box acts on position and on speed at once. Here is the mapping, with the boxes already built first:

Box As a function of θ As a function of ω What it is in software
wire, turn, riser θ ω identity, a rename, a routing
reverse −θ −ω negate
quarter (and any ratio box) θ/4 ω/4 multiply by a compile-time constant
tee, bus θ on two or three outputs same fan-out, copying a variable
meter, dial shows θ mod 360 shows ω by eye print, a debug probe
counter shows ∫ω dt = θ accumulated, in turns a register, an integrator
adder (differential) (θ₁ + θ₂)/2 (ω₁ + ω₂)/2 add, and with a reverse in front, subtract
selector (lever and idler) θ_in on one chosen output switch, routed by a human, not by a value
Geneva wheel floor(θ/360) × 90° (steps) pulses quantize, integer divide, sample-and-hold
ratchet running max of θ max(ω, 0) one-way, a latch, monotone clamp
cam and follower f(θ mod 360), any shape you draw lookup table in plastic
crank-slider or Scotch yoke sin θ as a sliding distance the built-in sine
slip clutch θ until torque > limit, then slips saturation, a fuse
end stop min(θ, θ_max) ω = 0 at the stop clamp, an assertion
escapement with pendulum releases one tooth per swing ω fixed by the pendulum a clock, the time base
governor (flyball) ω held near a set point negative feedback, a thermostat
flywheel low-pass filtered ω smoothing, momentum
spring store stores ∫ torque dθ, gives it back a capacitor or battery

Two things fall out of the table. First, position and speed are the same wire viewed twice: a ratio box scales both, an adder adds both, and only the counter and the dial choose which one you read. Second, the nonlinear boxes are where the physics lives. A gear train is a linear map and is boring on its own, exactly as y = 2x is; the Geneva, ratchet, clutch, escapement and governor are what make a machine behave.

Constants. There are three kinds, and all three are drivable:

  • A literal constant is a tooth ratio. It is baked into the box, like a const. The quarter box is x => x / 4.
  • A constant source is a drive. The crank is a human-rate source, the motor box is a roughly constant ω (it sags under load, like a weak power supply). A governor or an escapement turns a sloppy source into an exact one. That is the only way to get a true constant ω in this system, and it is a whole teaching unit by itself.
  • A settable constant, a fixed offset θ + c, is a differential whose second input is turned to the value you want and then held by a ratchet or a detent. Turn the knob to 3, lock it, and every downstream θ is 3 turns ahead. This is the one that most surprises people, because it shows that "a variable held still" and "a constant" are the same thing, which is also true of a register in a CPU.

What it cannot do, honestly. There is no comparator that routes on a value, so there is no if. The nearest thing is a trip: an end stop or a cam that flips a latch when θ reaches a threshold, which is an interrupt, not a branch. There is no multiply of two variables; Bush did it with a ball-and-disc integrator, and it is printable but it is a project, not a box. And there is no memory except the position of the shafts themselves, which is also why backlash matters so much: it is bit rot in the only register you have.

A catalog of fun boxes

Fourteen boxes, each built to teach one idea you can feel in your hand. Every one fits the standard as it stands (64 mm cell, module 1, lanes, four layers) unless the size column says otherwise. The cost column is the drag budget from section 5: meshes plus stacks, roughly. Build them in the order listed; the first five are the ones that turn the current set into a kit.

Three notes on the choices:

  • The planetary adder is the right MUX, not a bevel differential: a bevel set needs sideways axles and does not fit the 26 mm lid, while a ring gear round a sun sits flat on one layer. BOSL2 has ring_gear. Its constants come from the tooth counts (ring 48, sun 16, three 16-tooth planets is a classic set that meshes at module 1 and fits the cell).
  • The spring store, escapement and governor are the three that need parts other than gears: a strip of spring steel or a printed PETG spiral, a pendulum weight, and a friction pad. They are also the three that teach the most, because they are the only boxes where energy, not just motion, is the subject.
  • The cam and the Scotch yoke are cheap and visibly nonlinear. Pair either with the counter and you have a function plotter: turn the crank, read x on the counter and f(x) on the slider.

Five machines built from them

Each machine is a row or an L of boxes on the grid, driven from the west, and each one demonstrates one idea end to end. Costs are summed from the catalog so you can check them against the budget in section 5.

1. The adding machine (crank, crank, adder, counter; cost 11). Two cranks feed W and S of the adder. Turn one three times and the other five, and the counter reads 4.0, because the adder halves. Put a x2 box after it and it reads 8.0. Swap a reverse box in front of the second crank and it reads 3 − 5 = −2.0. This is the demo that makes the "functions" idea click, and it needs only one new box.

2. The clock (spring store, escapement, x1/4, x1/4, meter; cost 17). Wind the spring, release it, and the escapement lets it out one tick per pendulum swing. Two quarter boxes divide the ticks down and three dials show seconds, quarter minutes and minutes, like the hands of a clock: METER's two row dials and the one on its line. Change the pendulum length and watch every dial speed up. Teaches that time is counted, not measured.

3. Cruise control (motor, governor, slip clutch, counter; cost 13). The motor runs, the governor holds the speed, and the clutch is the load: tighten its knob and the line slows, then the governor opens up and it recovers. Loosen it and the governor throttles back. The counter shows the turns per minute staying steady while the load changes. This is the one feedback loop in the set and it is worth the governor's build cost by itself.

4. The harmonograph (crank, tee, Scotch yoke on E, x3 then Scotch yoke on N, a pen on a cross slide; cost 11). One crank, two yokes at frequencies 1 and 3, and a pen that takes x from one and y from the other draws a Lissajous figure on a card. Change the ratio box and the figure changes. Teaches frequency ratios and why 1:3 looks different from 2:3.

5. The fuse (crank, x1/4, x1/4, slip clutch, ratchet, trip flag; cost 18). Sixteen-to-one gearing means a light touch on the crank puts a hard shove into the clutch. The ratchet behind it holds whatever gets through, the trip flag goes up when the line reaches the stop, and from then on every turn of the crank slips the clutch and nothing downstream moves. Teaches torque multiplication, overload, and why a 16:1 reduction is not free power.

A sixth, the Blue Ball Machine itself: motor, BUS, a different ratio box on each of its two rows, and METER, whose two row dials and line dial turn at three speeds from one source. It is the clearest picture of what a line is, and it reuses boxes that exist today.

How far one drive really reaches

Three boxes is the right rule for a position reading and a conservative one for motion. A hand crank will turn 6 to 8 boxes of the current design, a small gearmotor 4 to 6, and both numbers roughly double with a drop of dry lube and bigger port gears. Here is the reasoning, so you can redo it after the first prints.

Where the drag comes from. Count a stage as one mesh plus one pin. Two losses, both proportional to load, and a small fixed floor:

  • Teeth. A dry printed module-1 mesh at 20 degrees runs at about 92 to 95 percent; call it 93. Steel gears in oil do 98, so the print surface costs about 5 points per mesh.
  • Pins. This one is the surprise. The pin carries the tooth force F plus the separating force F tan 20°, about 1.06 F. With PLA on PLA at μ ≈ 0.3 and a 2 mm pin radius, the friction torque is 0.3 × 1.06 F × 2 mm ≈ 0.64 mm × F, against a transmitted torque of F × 8 mm on a 16-tooth gear. That is an 8 percent loss per stack on a 16-tooth gear and 4 percent on a 32-tooth one. The plain printed pin costs more than the teeth do.
  • Floor. A clean stack with 0.2 mm of play has almost no fixed drag (its weight is grams). A stack that rubs on a keeper, a string or a lid adds 1 to 3 mNm each, which is why the first print matters more than the arithmetic.

So one 16-tooth stage passes about 0.93 × 0.92 ≈ 0.86 of its torque, and a typical box has 2 to 3 stages (wire 2, tee 3, counter 4 with the carry). Ten boxes is 25 stages and 0.86²⁵ ≈ 2 percent, which sounds hopeless until you remember the chain drives nothing but its own friction. What the drive feels is the sum of each stage's floor divided by the efficiency between it and the drive. With a 0.5 mNm floor and 0.86 per stage:

Boxes downstream Stages Crank torque needed Verdict
3 8 about 8 mNm effortless
6 15 about 25 mNm easy, a finger on the knob
8 20 about 50 mNm the limit of comfortable cranking
10 25 about 100 mNm two fingers and a frown

A thumb wheel with a knob 12 mm out from the center takes 3 to 5 N comfortably, which is 40 to 60 mNm. So a crank reaches 6 boxes with ease and 8 at a push. A box that rubs anywhere cuts a box or two off that.

The 9 V motor. A bare DC motor is useless here: 10 000 rpm and about 1 mNm. It must be a gearmotor, and the motor box gears it down again so the port runs at 30 to 60 rpm, which is the speed that looks like a machine rather than a blur. The motor box as built (see docs/boxes/motor.md) uses an N20 12 V, 150 rpm gearmotor on a 9 V PP3, about 110 rpm at the shaft. Inside, a 10-tooth bevel pinion drives a 15-tooth bevel gear, then 12 drives 40, then 32-tooth gears drive the 16-tooth E and N ports, so the ports run at about 44 rpm with roughly x2.5 torque gain overall, both at x1 from one shaft like the crank. Stall at a port works out to about 140 to 200 mNm; plan on 35 to 50 mNm continuous. That reaches 5 to 7 boxes by the table, and unlike a hand it does not push harder when something sticks, so plan on 4 to 6. A PP3 alkaline holds about 550 mAh, which is a few hours of running.

Backlash is the other limit, and it only bites on reversal. The standard allows about 0.2 mm per mesh at the pitch line, which on a 16-tooth gear is 1.4 degrees. Eight meshes is 11 degrees, a third of a tenths digit on the counter; sixteen is 23 degrees. Driven one way, backlash is a constant offset you zero out once; reversed, it is dead travel that the counter shows as the number lagging. That is the real reason to keep readouts within three boxes of the drive, and why the adding machine works (it never reverses) while a bidirectional one would need anti-backlash gears.

Wear. Dry PLA on PLA at 50 rpm polishes within a few hours and then settles; wear widens backlash rather than stopping anything. Pins wear faster than teeth because they carry the whole load on a small area. Expect a motor-driven layout to need re-zeroing after a weekend, not to seize.

Mitigations, in order of payoff.

  1. A torque budget in the standard. Give every box a cost in stages (wire 2, tee 3, quarter 3, counter 4, Geneva 3, and so on) and every drive a budget (crank 20, motor box 15, to be set from the first prints). The rule: along any path from a drive, the summed cost of the boxes may not exceed the budget. It reads like TTL fan-out and it replaces "three boxes" with a number that is right for each chain. Add a second rule for readings: a dial or counter must be within 8 meshes of its drive, or its lid says "±".
  2. A power line on N/S. The drive already feeds N. Run its column north and give each row a tap box, S in and E out, the mirror of the turn box. A tap restarts a row with fresh torque at x1. It is a repeater only for the x1 signal, which is most of them, and it lets hungry boxes (the counter's carry, a Geneva, a clutch) draw from the drive directly instead of through five neighbors. A true repeater that preserves an arbitrary ratio and phase is a servo, which is a project; for that case a second motor box is the honest answer.
  3. Lubricate the pins. A PTFE dry film or a trace of silicone grease takes μ from 0.3 to about 0.1, which cuts the pin loss from 8 to 3 percent and takes the 8-box crank torque from 50 to about 35 mNm. Note it in the assembly steps; it is the cheapest win there is.
  4. Bigger port gears where there is room. A 32-tooth port halves the pin loss. BUS and METER use 16-tooth ports on lane B, so they cost no more than any other box.
  5. A metal pin option. A 4 mm brass or steel rod pressed into the shell gives μ ≈ 0.2 against PLA and never wears. Keep the printed pin as the default and offer the rod on drive boxes and taps, where the load is.
  6. Do not go to module 1.5. The teeth are not the problem: at these torques a module-1 PLA tooth carries 20 times its load. A bigger module would cost lane capacity and buy nothing. Keep module 1.

A playful look: color by role

Color the role of a part, not the box, so a child can follow a signal across a table of boxes without reading a lid: energy comes in at red, enters each box at blue, leaves at orange, and is read at green. The keys already enforce "output meets input"; color makes the rule visible before it is felt, which is the whole teaching trick. Six colors, chosen from Bambu PLA Basic so they are one click away:

Role Parts Color Why
Structure shell, lid, keepers, sleeves Bone (off-white, warm) a quiet background so the moving parts pop; reads as a toy, not a tool
Drive crank wheel and knob, the motor box lid, a spring store's winder Red energy enters here; one red thing per machine
Input the W and S port gears, and the lever on a selector Blue parameters, what a box is given
Output the E and N port gears Orange return values; orange always meets blue across a joint
Display dials, digit wheels, window frames, a cam follower's tip, a flag Green where you read a value; green is never driven by hand
Transmission every inner gear, the pins White the body of the function; nobody needs to look at it

Lid text and arrows go in black on the bone lid, replacing the yellow-on-slate of the pocket tools. Keep slate and yellow for the pocket tools; they belong to that series and the two should not look like one family.

How the code supports teaching:

  • Tracing. "Find the red. Follow orange to blue, box by box, until you reach green." That sentence is a whole lesson on dataflow, and it is the first thing to try with the demo layout.
  • Types. E/W and N/S ports are both blue or orange; the layers already keep them apart. Do not add a color for the channel, it would double the palette for a rule the keys already enforce.
  • Constants. A settable constant (the adder's latched input) gets a red knob, because turning it is putting energy in. A compile-time constant (a ratio) is just white gears, invisible, which is right.
  • Function on the lid. Keep the name on the lid and add the box's function as a symbol beside it, in black at 8 mm: x¼, +, ∫ (counter), → (wire), ↺ (reverse), ⌈⌉ (Geneva), ⇀ (ratchet). A lid becomes a docstring, and a row of lids reads as an expression.

Printing it. The P1S has four AMS slots, and the export writes one 3MF per part, so six colors is no problem: print shells and lids in a bone-and-black job, gears and wheels in a white, blue, orange, green job, and the few red parts with either. A box never needs more than four colors in one job. If you want to cut it to four for one combined print, merge display into orange (outputs you read) and drive into blue (an input from a hand), which keeps the trace rule intact.

Less industrial, beyond color. Three cheap changes to the look: round the lid corners to a 6 mm radius and the window corners to 2 mm; make the windows larger (the counter's are tight) with a raised lip in green; and give every drive a knob you want to grab, a 10 mm sphere in red rather than a cylinder. None of these touch the standard.

What to change, and what to build next

Four edits to STANDARD.md and a build order. Each is small and none breaks a box that exists.

  • [ ] Torque budget section. Define a stage (one mesh plus one pin), give every box a cost and every drive a budget, and state the two rules: path cost ≤ budget, and readouts within 8 meshes of their drive. Start with crank 20 and motor 15 and correct them from the weight test already in the roadmap.
  • [ ] Power line. Promote the roadmap's N/S power idea to a rule: a drive feeds N at x1, and a tap box (S in, E out) is the standard way to restart a row. Add tap to the catalog beside turn; it is the same box mirrored across the diagonal.
  • [ ] Assembly note. One line: a PTFE dry film on every pin before the gears go on.
  • [ ] Colors. Replace the pocket-tools palette with the six-role code from section 6, and move the filament slot numbers into tools/export_3mf.sh per part role.

Build order, by what each unlocks: adder first (it is the one new box that makes a machine out of the existing set, and it settles the MUX question), then Geneva and ratchet (both are one afternoon and both are nonlinear, which is where the demos get interesting), then the slip clutch, then the motor box from the other thread with a tap beside it. Leave the escapement, governor and spring store for after the first prints have given a real drag number, because all three depend on it.

One measurement would sharpen every number in this document: print the demo row, hang a weight from the crank knob at a known radius, and find the lightest weight that keeps the chain turning. That gives the per-stage efficiency and the floor directly, and it is the first test the roadmap already lists.
