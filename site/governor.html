GOVERNOR ⊛

A speed limit. Put it on a branch of a line, and the line turns freely when slow but can't run away when fast. The green rotor on the lid spins 6.25 times as fast as the line; fast enough, its two shoes fly out and rub the drum around it. A SPRING run through a governor turns at a steady pace instead of whizzing round in a second.

3 stages while it's slow. Fast, the brake adds as much as it takes to hold the speed, and that's its job.

How it works

The rotor has two shoes, each holding a 6 mm steel ball, on thin arms (0.8 mm) that curl round the hub.

Spinning pulls the shoes outward and the arms bend. At about 30 turns a second of the rotor (4 to 5 of the line), the shoes have moved the 0.8 mm to the drum and start to rub.

The pull grows with the square of the speed, so a little faster means a lot more drag: the speed hardly rises past that point.

The 6.25× step-up makes that happen at a speed a line actually reaches.

Why it is built this way

  • A centrifugal brake, not a Watt governor. At this size a hinged flyball linkage would be fiddly and stiff, and its balls would barely lift. A shoe brake does the same job with one printed part: it's the governor inside a rotary telephone dial.
  • The rotor is on the lid, so you can watch it, and swap it for one with stiffer or softer arms.
  • Steel balls, not printed ones. A 6 mm steel ball weighs about three times an 8 mm printed one, so the brake works at a lower speed.
  • A strip of felt round the inside of the drum makes it quieter and smoother, and closes the gap so the brake starts at a lower speed.

How to assemble it

  1. Wipe a PTFE dry film on every pin. The pin is the bigger loss in a stage, and the lube roughly halves it.
  2. Build it in this order: port W, then A, B and G; keeper on W and the lid on; press the two balls into the rotor's pockets, then press the rotor onto G's D-key inside the drum.
  3. Put a keeper on each side that has ports: its legs go in the notches and its flange in the recess on the wall top.
  4. Put the lid on. Its corner holes take the pins on the corner blocks.

To join it to a neighbor, lower it onto the neighbor's tongues from above.

What it teaches

  • Centrifugal force grows with the square of the speed, so a brake that uses it holds a speed almost exactly.
  • Steam engines (the Watt governor), telephone dials, music boxes and wind-up toys all hold their speed with one.
  • Gearing up multiplies the speed, so a small rotor can do a big job.
