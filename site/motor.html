MOTOR ⚡

A 9 V battery runs a small gearmotor lying flat inside a two-cell box. A bevel pair turns the drive up into the gears, and the box feeds E and N the way the crank does. A toggle on the red lid runs it forward, off or reverse.

2×1 cells · in: none · out: E, N · budget 15

Torque cost

Drive budget: 0 stages

Budget 15 stages. The motor gives roughly 75 to 110 mNm stall on 9 V; after the bevel and two spur stages that is 140 to 200 mNm stall at a port. A small DC motor runs comfortably near a quarter of stall, so plan on 35 to 50 mNm continuous, enough for 4 to 6 boxes. Check the stall figure of the motor you buy, as they vary by seller.

How it works

The N20 gearmotor lies flat along the north side with its shaft pointing east. A 10-tooth bevel pinion pressed on the shaft meshes a 15-tooth bevel gear on stack B, which turns the drive from horizontal to vertical.

B's 12-tooth spur drives the 40-tooth gear on stack K (layer 3). K carries 32-tooth gears on layers 1 and 2, which drive E and N exactly like the crank's stack.

The 9 V battery lies in the south of the west cell. A DPDT ON-OFF-ON toggle reverses the battery's polarity across the motor: FWD, OFF, REV.

Overall the box slows the motor about 2.5 times and multiplies its torque by the same amount (1.5 × 3.33 × 0.5).

Wiring

The motor runs off the battery through one DPDT ON-OFF-ON toggle, which swaps the polarity to reverse it. The full circuit, the parts and the safety notes are in Electrical below.

Electrical: battery, switch and motor

The motor box is the only box with electronics in it. There are five parts: a 9 V battery, a resettable fuse, a reversing switch, the motor and an optional capacitor. Nothing else is needed. There is no circuit board.

How it works. A DPDT switch is two switches that move together (poles A and B). Each pole has a middle pin (M) and two end pins (W and E). The battery goes to the E pins: red (+) to A-E and black (−) to B-E. Two crossover wires copy them to the far W pins the other way round, + to B-W and − to A-W. The motor sits across the two middle pins.

A toggle's lever points away from the pins it joins. With the lever over at FWD (west), the middle pins are touching the east pins. The crossovers can never short the battery, because each middle pin touches only one end pin at a time.

Where it sits in the box

  • The battery lies flat in the south of the west cell, terminals east. Its snap sits at the east end, just west of the gears.
  • The motor lies along the north side with its tabs at the west end, behind the spacer.
  • The switch hangs from its nut in the lid, over the north-west corner, and its body drops into a cradle in the shell that stops it turning. The lid carries the switch, so the switch's wires tie the lid to the box. Cut them long enough that the lid can lie upside down beside the west end.
  • Wires cross the top of the battery, which has about 4 mm of room under the lid, and come up to the switch from the south. Keep every wire west of the motor's gearbox. A wire caught in a gear is the most likely way to get a short.

Mark motor lead 1 with a dot of marker so you can tell the two blue leads apart.

Why F1. A 9 V battery is safe to touch, but if its two leads touch each other, an amp or more flows. That makes thin wire and the battery hot enough to soften PLA. The PTC fuse in the red lead trips in a few seconds and resets once it has cooled. In use it only drops about 0.3 V, so the motor runs a few percent slower. A box without it will still work, but F1 is cheap insurance in a toy with wires next to gears.

Why C1 is optional. It soaks up the sparks from the motor's brushes. You only need it if a radio (the BRAIN boxes) or a sensor nearby misbehaves. It must be ceramic. An electrolytic capacitor has a + and a −, and the switch reverses the motor.

Which way is forward

The standard says forward is counter-clockwise seen from above at the output ports. In this box that means the motor shaft turns clockwise when you look at the end of the shaft (from the east, at the pinion). The bevel pair turns that into counter-clockwise on B (seen from above), B → K reverses it, and K → port reverses it back.

N20 sellers don't agree on which tab is +, and the number of stages in the motor's own gearbox decides which way the shaft turns. So don't trust the markings. Test it:

  1. Before the gears go in, wire everything, snap on the battery and push the lever to FWD. Look at the shaft end. It should turn clockwise.
  2. If it turns counter-clockwise, swap the two motor leads on the middle pins (A-M and B-M). Leave the battery and crossover wires alone.
  3. With the gears in, FWD turns E and N counter-clockwise seen from above, OFF stops them, and REV turns them clockwise.
  4. Run it for a minute with your hand on the motor and the fuse. Warm is fine, hot is not.

Safety notes

  • Polarity. Red is +, black is −, everywhere. On the battery, the small round terminal is + and the larger six-sided one is −. Getting the motor leads the wrong way round can't hurt anything: it only runs backward until you swap them.
  • No parts with a + and a − (diodes, electrolytic capacitors) go across the motor, because the switch reverses it.
  • Insulate everything. Heat-shrink every joint on the switch lugs, the jumpers, the fuse and the motor tabs. No bare metal should show anywhere.
  • Stalls. If a train jams, the motor hums and pushes with its full stall torque, 140 to 200 mNm at a port. That is more than printed teeth should carry for long. Switch it OFF at once. A long stall may also trip F1, which resets after a minute with the switch OFF.
  • Off and out. Switch OFF before you open the lid. Take the battery out to store the box for weeks, because an old alkaline PP3 can leak.
  • Stay at 9 V. A 12 V supply runs the motor at its rated speed, about 60 rpm at the ports, with about a third more stall torque. That is beyond what the torque budget assumes.

Other boxes with motors

BRAIN and CBDRIVE (brain.md) use the same N20 and bevel train, two of them per box. Their electronics are different. A DRV8833 driver reverses the motors in software, and the power comes from a 1S LiPo through an SPST PWR toggle. Their wiring is described in brain.md, not here.

Why it is built this way

  • Bevel gears, not a worm. A printed PLA worm keeps only about a quarter of the torque when dry. A bevel pair keeps about as much as a spur mesh.
  • Lying flat. Standing up, the motor would need a taller box. Lying down it fits the standard 21.6 mm interior, and the bevel pair costs one stage.
  • Two cells. The battery, switch and motor need the space. The N port sits on the east cell so the power line runs up from it.
  • Red lid. Only a motor's lid is red: the lid says the energy comes from here.

How to assemble it

  1. Wire it as in Electrical and run the direction test there. Then press the bevel pinion onto the motor's shaft (hub 14.5 mm from the gearbox face); fit ports E and N, then B (bevel down), then K (40 teeth on top), then K's sleeve and the keepers. Lower the motor into its cradle 3.5 mm west, slide it east into mesh and drop the spacer behind it. The slot up the spacer's middle clears the motor's lower terminal tab and its solder, so the spacer presses only on the sides of the end cap. Fit the switch through the lid with its nut, wire it, snap on the battery, close the lid.

To join it to a neighbor, lower it onto the neighbor's tongues from above.

What it teaches

  • Electric to mechanical: a motor is fast and weak, and a gearbox trades speed for torque.
  • Bevel gears turn a drive through a right angle.
  • Reversing a DC motor by swapping polarity with a DPDT switch.
  • Efficiency: every stage loses a little, so the motor's budget is smaller than the crank's.
