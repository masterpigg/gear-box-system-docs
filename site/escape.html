TICK ⏱

An escapement lets a driven line through one tooth at a time, tick, tock, at a steady beat. Put it on a branch of a line driven by SPRING and the whole line runs at the foliot's pace instead of all at once. With a counter on another branch, that makes a clock.

W (16) → C (32 on layer 1, with a 30-tooth escape wheel on layer 4). The anchor turns on a pin 24 mm north of C; its shaft comes up through the lid and carries the foliot. Each swing there and back lets one tooth past: 12° of the escape wheel, 24° of W. An end box: it regulates whatever line it's on.

Torque cost

2 of the crank's 20 stages · 2 of the motor's 15

2 stages for the line, plus the energy the foliot takes each swing. That's the point: the escapement spends the drive's energy in small, regular amounts.

How it works

The wheel and the anchor. The anchor's two pallets reach into the wheel's teeth, 6.5 teeth apart. Each pallet has a sloped face, 3 mm long.

Impulse. The wheel pushes a tooth up one pallet's slope, and that swings the anchor. The tooth slips off the end of the face, and the wheel jumps forward about 2.6° (the drop) until a tooth meets the other pallet.

Recoil. The swing is carrying the other pallet in, so that tooth gets pushed back a little while it slows the foliot and turns it round. Then it drives the foliot back the other way.

The beat. One tooth goes past for every swing there and back. The foliot's weight sets how long a swing takes: put the steel balls further out for a slower beat.

This is the oldest clock escapement. The verge and foliot of the first mechanical clocks worked the same way, with a heavy bar instead of a pendulum, and it works flat on a table.

Why it is built this way

  • A foliot, not a pendulum. A pendulum has to hang, and one hanging off the lid's edge would be shorter than the box is tall. The foliot swings level on top.
  • The pallets come from a simulation. tools/esc_sim.py follows the wheel through every swing angle and found faces that advance it exactly one tooth a cycle without ever letting it run free. The 39 states the fit check uses are its output.
  • The pallets taper to their inner ends, so the tooth coming up behind passes under them.
  • One cell, not two. The spec asked for a 1×2 box to make room for a pendulum; the foliot doesn't need it.

How to assemble it

  1. Wipe a PTFE dry film on every pin. The pin is the bigger loss in a stage, and the lube roughly halves it.
  2. Build it in this order: port W, then C (PTFE on the pins and the pallet faces); lower the anchor onto its pin with its pallets either side of the wheel's top teeth, turning C if a tooth is in the way; keeper on W and the lid on; press the foliot onto the anchor's D-key and set the two balls in matching pockets; drive W gently and give the foliot a nudge.
  3. Put a keeper on each side that has ports: its legs go in the notches and its flange in the recess on the wall top.
  4. Put the lid on. Its corner holes take the pins on the corner blocks.

To join it to a neighbor, lower it onto the neighbor's tongues from above.

What it teaches

  • An escapement turns a steady push into equal steps. Every mechanical clock has one, and so do music boxes and clockwork toys.
  • The oscillator sets the rate: move the balls out and the beat slows.
  • A foliot has no spring of its own, so a harder push still speeds it up. That's why clockmakers moved to the pendulum and the balance spring, which keep their own time.
